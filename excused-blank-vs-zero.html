Excused vs blank vs zero: three different cells, three different grades

A blank cell is "not scored yet"; the word excused is "not scored, and marked excused"; a typed 0 is a real zero that counts. The first two are left out of the average. The third is not — and the difference is large.

The swing, in numbers

A student has four homework scores of 90. Add a fifth cell and watch the homework average move:

Fifth cellMeaningHomework average
blanknot scored yet90.00
excusednot scored, excused90.00
0scored zero72.00

Four 90s with a blank: (90+90+90+90) ÷ 4 = 90.00. Four 90s with a 0: (90+90+90+90+0) ÷ 5 = 72.00. One cell — an 18.00-point swing in the category average.

And it compounds into the final. If homework is 20% of the grade, that 18-point category swing moves the final by 3.60 points (18.00 × 20%). A single blank-versus-zero decision can move a final across a letter-band boundary, on one cell.

Why the formula can't tell the difference

AVERAGE() skips both blanks and text like "excused", and counts a 0. That's the entire mechanism. The problem is that a blank and an excused cell are intentions, not values — the spreadsheet can store "this is missing" but not "this is missing on purpose".

The three rules, stated plainly

  • Blank — the work exists but hasn't been scored. Leave it out of both the numerator and the denominator, so it neither helps nor hurts.
  • Excused — the work does not exist for this student. Leave it out exactly like a blank, but keep the word excused so the report card shows the difference between "missing" and "excused".
  • Zero — the work was scored as zero. Count it. This is the only one of the three that lowers the average.

Three ways this goes wrong

1. Blank silently treated as zero

The most damaging bug: a template that fills empty cells with 0 before averaging. A student who missed a day of data collection gets a 0 for work that was never graded. Gradebooks that import blanks as zeros do this invisibly.

2. "E" or "X" guessed as excused

Some teachers write E for excused, others for absent, others for exempt. Guessing converts one of those into a different meaning and silently changes a grade. Gradewright refuses to guess: a cell must be a number, blank, or the word excused (or ex, any case). Anything else — including a lone e or E, which could mean absent or exempt — raises an error that names the cell.

3. Report card can't say "excused"

If the source sheet drops the distinction between blank and excused, the printed card shows a missing assignment with no explanation, and a parent reasonably reads it as "didn't do it".

When a spreadsheet formula is the better answer. If you already have the discipline to type 0 for zeros and excused for excused, AVERAGE() behaves correctly and you don't need anything else. Gradewright earns its place when the sheet is shared or inherited and the cell-by-cell discipline isn't guaranteed — it distinguishes the three and tells you where a cell is ambiguous.
