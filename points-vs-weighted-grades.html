Points vs weighted grades: which one is your sheet calculating?

Total-points grading divides everything earned by everything possible. Weighted-category grading averages each category first, then applies weights. They answer different questions about what a missed homework is worth, and the same scores can come out differently under each.

One student, two methods

Homework 20%, quizzes 30%, exams 50%. Homework scores 90/80/100, quizzes 70/85/95, exams 88/100 and 76/100.

MethodHow it worksResult
Weighted categoriesaverage each category, apply 20/30/5084.00
Total points684 earned ÷ 800 possible85.50

Same scores, two different finals. The 1.50-point gap isn't a rounding error — it's two different policies about how much one homework is worth.

The weighted arithmetic:

90.00 × 20% + 83.33 × 30% + 82.00 × 50% = 84.00

The total-points arithmetic:

earned  = 90 + 80 + 100 + 70 + 85 + 95 + 88 + 76 = 684
possible = 300 + 300 + 200 = 800
final    = 684 ÷ 800 = 85.50

Which one your sheet is doing

  • You average each category, then combine them → weighted categories.
  • You add up every score and divide by the total possible → total points.
  • You're not sure → look for a SUM(...)/SUM(...) pattern, or a grade that changes when you add a low-value assignment to a category.

Three ways the distinction bites

1. A single big assignment distorts total points

Under total points, a 200-point project carries twice the weight of a 100-point exam, whether or not you meant it to. Weighted categories make the intended weight explicit, so a category's weight stays fixed no matter how many points the assignments inside it total.

2. Points-based exams mixed into percentage categories

The common real-world case: homework is a percentage, exams are points. Averaging exam points naively (AVERAGE(42,76) for a 50-point and a 100-point exam) gives 59.00 instead of the correct 78.67. Mixing the two encodings in one sheet is where most grade formulas break.

3. Parents can't reproduce the number

Both methods are defensible; the failure is when the method is implicit. A parent who adds up the points and gets 85.50 when the card says 84.00 deserves an explanation, and the card should say which method produced it.

When a spreadsheet formula is the better answer. A single, uniform method — all points, no category weights — is a one-cell SUM()/SUM() and needs no tool.
